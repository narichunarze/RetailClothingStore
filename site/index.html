01

The problem

“We run out of M and L on our best sellers, and end the season discounting XS and XL by 50%. We find out too late.”

Head of Operations (simulated)

  • 12 stores in Peru, Chile, Colombia and Mexico
  • ~760 SKUs (model × color × size)
  • 8 h/week of manual Excel consolidation
  • 0 demand forecasts; weekly replenishment “by eye”

02

The finding: it's the size curve, not the volume

The planner splits every order evenly across sizes. Demand isn't even: M and L make up more than half of it, yet each gets the same share of stock as XS.

05

Results

Lost-sales estimator validated against ground truth

The first version overestimated by 60%: inventory snapshots are end-of-day, so a SKU that sold out mid-day counted as a full lost day. After the fix it's within ~8% of the truth.

Genie agent: 6 → 9 → 10 → 12 out of 12

Half of the early failures were in my reference SQL, not in the agent. Business definitions were written into the instructions, so the score didn't come from overfitting to the benchmark.

Trained on uncensored demand

Sales are censored on stockout days. The model learns from sales plus estimated lost sales, otherwise it would under-forecast exactly the sizes that run out.

06

AI-first, with judgment

Built with Claude Code. Every AI-generated piece was checked with tests and validated against ground truth. Some of what that caught:

Stockout death spiral

The first replenishment simulation reordered from already-censored sales. Lost demand was 3× actual sales.

Inflated lost sales

The estimator was 60% too high. Validating it against the generator's ground truth exposed the end-of-day snapshot issue.

Decimal → pandas

Spark decimals became Python Decimal objects and broke the forecast math. Caught in a local end-to-end run, before production.

Production I/O errors

Jobs intermittently failed to read code from the workspace. Fixed by shipping it as a versioned wheel from a Unity Catalog volume.
